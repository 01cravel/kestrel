Plain-English method

How Kestrel reaches a view—in normal language

1. Is it a good business?Kestrel checks sales, profits, debt and the return the company earns on its money.
2. Is the share too expensive?A brilliant company can still be a poor investment if its price already assumes perfection.
3. Is it improving?Recent business results, forecasts and share-price direction show whether the story is strengthening or weakening.
4. What could hurt you?Debt, sharp price swings and owning too much of one thing can turn a good idea into a bad portfolio decision.
5. Can the facts be trusted?Official, current and independently checked evidence earns more confidence. Gaps and conflicts reduce it.
6. What does the rating mean?Buy means “worth considering with the evidence available.” Hold is the normal answer. Nothing here is a promise or an automatic trade.

The score ranks one company against another; it is not the chance of making a profit. Missing information never earns points. Hold is deliberately the normal answer.

Evidence before confidence

Which information Kestrel trusts most

Every important number keeps a receipt: where it came from, what date it describes, when Kestrel received it, its unit and its currency. Calculations can interpret facts; they cannot invent them.

1 · Official recordsYour broker, regulators, stock exchanges, companies and government bodies establish the strongest facts.
2 · Professional market dataSpecialist price, dividend, takeover, forecast and risk records help verify and standardise those facts.
3 · Free market feedsFinnhub, FMP and Yahoo can provide an early view, but cannot support the strongest confidence alone.
4 · ExperimentsModels and unusual data can suggest what to research. They can never establish a fact by themselves.

Missing evidence never counts as positive evidence. Conflicts lower confidence automatically.
